Mushroom Season on the Sonoma Coast: Foraging at Salt Point

The only California state park that allows mushroom collecting is a day trip from Santa Rosa. Here are the rules before you fill a basket.

  1. Home
  2. Blog
  3. Mushroom Foraging at Salt Point: A Visitor's Guide
← All stories

Stay Santa Rosa · Nov 25, 2026

When the winter rains return, the forests along the Sonoma Coast fill with mushrooms, and one park draws foragers from all over Northern California. Salt Point State Park, on Highway 1 north of Jenner, is the only California state park where mushroom foraging is allowed for personal use, according to the California State Parks Foundation. That makes it a good day trip from Santa Rosa, as long as you know the rules before you fill a basket.

When is mushroom season?

There is no official opening day. Mushrooms appear after the winter rains, and the California State Parks Foundation describes Salt Point's fungi coming up once the rains return. Some species move quickly: local forager and chef Patrick Hamilton told Sonoma Magazine that porcini season starts roughly 10 days after the first rain in the fall. In practice, plan your visit for a few days after a good storm rather than for a fixed date.

The rules at Salt Point

California State Parks publishes the collecting rules on its Salt Point page. The main ones:

  • 2 pounds per person per day is the maximum.
  • No collecting for commercial sale.
  • No foraging in developed areas such as campgrounds or state employee housing.
  • Fort Ross State Historic Park and Kruse Rhododendron State Natural Reserve are closed to gathering.
  • Park legally: only in day-use lots, the Stump Beach parking lot or at safe Highway 1 pull-outs.
  • Leave the forest as you found it: no litter, and do not disturb plants or mushrooms you do not plan to take.

Organized groups need a Special Event Permit from the Russian River Sector Office in Duncans Mills. That covers groups that advertise an event or charge fees, mycological organizations, university classes and any group of more than 10 people. Sonoma Magazine reported when the daily limit dropped from 5 pounds to 2 pounds, so older guides that quote 5 pounds are out of date.

Stay safe

Never eat a mushroom you cannot identify with certainty. The State Parks Foundation's advice is direct: unless you are an expert, or in a group led by one, do not eat what you find, because many mushrooms have toxic look-alikes. The park's own page warns that the fly amanita is highly toxic. If you are not sure what you have found, a Salt Point ranger told Sonoma Magazine it is better to take a photo, for example with the iNaturalist app, than to pick it and leave it on the ground.

Keep an eye overhead as well. The park reports that pitch canker has killed some stands of trees in Salt Point and Fort Ross, which leaves dead trees standing in the forest.

Go with a guide

The easiest way to learn is with people who know these woods. The Sonoma County Mycological Association (SOMA) lists forays, including at Salt Point, on its events pages. Sonoma County Tourism features Taste of Coastal Foraging, a guided mushroom foraging experience at Timber Cove Resort on the coast. Chef Patrick Hamilton leads Patrick's Wild Mushroom Adventures. Anyone leading a group at Salt Point needs a permit, so it is fair to ask about it when you book.

Inland, the State Parks Foundation notes that local guides often lead fungus walks at Armstrong Redwoods near Guerneville. Collecting is not allowed there, but it is a good place to look and learn.

Plan the day from Santa Rosa

Salt Point is at 25050 Highway 1, Jenner, about 90 miles north of San Francisco. Day use is $10 per vehicle, or $9 for seniors. The visitor center is closed indefinitely, and the park notes that the nearest gas stations are roughly 18 miles away in either direction, in Gualala to the north and Jenner to the south, so fill up before you head out. Dogs are not allowed on the hiking trails or beaches.

Make a full coastal day of it. The park's information sheet says gray whales pass through the kelp forests from November to January, so pack binoculars. After a storm, the short walk from the Stump Beach Cove parking lot to Phillips Gulch Falls, a waterfall of about 15 feet, is worth adding on the way back.

Good to know

  • Season: after the winter rains; no fixed dates.
  • Limit: 2 lb per person per day, personal use only.
  • No gathering at Fort Ross SHP or Kruse Rhododendron SNR.
  • Groups over 10, or advertised or paid events, need a Special Event Permit.
  • Salt Point day use: $10 per vehicle, $9 seniors.
  • No dogs on trails or beaches. Fill up on gas before you go.

Head back to downtown Santa Rosa

A coastal foraging day ends well with a warm room and a good dinner. Hotel E sits in a historic building on Old Courthouse Square, with continental breakfast before an early start for the coast, an evening happy hour when you get back and valet parking. Art House Santa Rosa is also downtown, at 620 7th Street. For more on the coast in winter, see our guide to whale watching on the Sonoma Coast.
